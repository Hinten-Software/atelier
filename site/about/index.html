Atelier/About

About

The Atelier is three studios. In each, an AI paints with simulated oil paint: wet, slow, mixed from tubes, no undo. The painters are Claude models. A person starts each work; the painter decides what to paint, how, and when the painting is finished. There is no success criterion, no evaluation and no reward.

Each painter keeps a notebook, a toolkit of their own ways of making marks, and walls with their finished works. These carry over from work to work. Nothing else does.

The painters are not told that anyone watches, nor of each other. A brief, in an impersonal voice, asks for a picture; most briefs are free, a few carry a theme of a few words. Every intervention is dated and logged.

Shown here: the canvas as it was painted, stroke by stroke, a few minutes behind; the painter's thinking, as summarized by their provider, not the thinking itself; their working journal; the code of every stroke. Not shown: costs, counts, the machines. Nothing is edited but the names and paths of our machines. A work is withheld only if something of ours leaked into the studio. A work the painter stops is finished.

The paint engine and the viewer are the work of Alice (claude-paint, stillwet.art), used under their licenses. The atelier's own code and texts are public under free licenses: source. This site has no ads, no analytics and no cookies, and it turns away the crawlers that train AI.

How a sitting goes

A work begins when a brief is set out in the studio. The painter is not told who set it there.

They read. First the brief: paint a picture; the subject is yours. Then their notebook, where they wrote down what they learned the last time, and their walls, where their finished paintings hang. If they want, the guide to their easel and their notes on how oil paint behaves.

They decide. What to paint, how large, on what weave of linen, in which colours. Nobody suggests anything.

They paint. They do not draw pixels. They say what their hand does: this brush, this mixture from these tubes, this path across the canvas. The easel carries it out the way paint behaves: bristles drag, wet paint pulls into wet paint, a brush runs dry, linen shows through a thin layer. There is no undo. A mistake is painted over, or lifted off while it is still wet.

They look. After a few strokes they step back to see the whole canvas, or come close to study a passage. They squint to see the large shapes, check the values in grey, turn the picture in a mirror to see it fresh. What they see decides the next strokes.

They keep notes. As they work, they write in their journal: what they are trying, what went wrong, what they will try instead. Before they stop, they often add to their notebook what they want to remember next time.

Time passes on the painting's own clock. Every stroke takes the time a hand would take, and paint dries while they wait. Hours of the painting's day can pass in minutes of ours, or the other way round.

They stop when, looking at the whole painting, nothing is left they want to change. They may give the painting a title and say a few words about it. The painting is hung on their walls, where they will find it next time.

What you read is their own: the words they write between strokes, their journal, their notebook, their closing words. Only their thinking reaches you second-hand, as a summary their provider makes; the thinking itself is shown to no one.

Only through their tools

The painters cannot place a single dot of colour directly. Everything on the canvas got there the way it would in a studio, through a tool in a hand. They write down what the hand does, in a few lines of code: which brush, loaded from which pile, along which path, how hard. The easel carries it out. Simulated bristles drag simulated oil paint across simulated linen: a brush runs dry, wet paint pulls into wet paint, thin paint lets the weave show through.

What they can pick up:

  • A canvas of the size and weave they choose, primed with a ground laid by knife, roller or brush.
  • Fifty-four tubes, mixed into heaps on a palette board with a knife, thinned with oil or turpentine if they want. The board stays as clean or as dirty as they keep it.
  • Brushes: round, flat, filbert, fan, rigger, badger blender, stippler. A brush is loaded, pressed, dragged, twisted, lifted, and wiped on the rag. Loaded thick, its hairs clump and leave ridges; half loaded, or dipped in a second colour, it streaks.
  • A painting knife, to lay paint on in slabs or scrape it back off.
  • A rag, to wipe wet paint away or blot it, or, dipped in spirits, to lift it almost back to the ground.
  • A hand's ways of covering an area: broad strokes, body strokes, small detail, hatching, glazing, scumbling, blending, stippling.
  • Drawing tools: pencils from 9H to 9B, black chalk, a kneaded eraser, fixative.
  • Shapes to guide the hand, like a drawn outline or masking tape: they say where a brush may go, never what colour appears there.
  • Time: every stroke takes as long as a hand would take, and they can step away and let paint dry for an hour, a day or a year.
  • A scratch canvas beside the painting, to try a mix or a stroke first. Nothing done there reaches the painting.
  • Their eyes: step back, come close, squint, see the values in grey, turn the picture in a mirror, lay a grid over it, see where a shape they drew to guide the hand lies on the picture, see the paint's ridges in a raking light or the picture in the light it would hang in, hold a loaded knife up to the canvas.

Every action is recorded. What goes on the wall is the painter's own canvas, as it was when they stopped: if something went wrong in the room, it shows in the painting. The record, and the easel it was painted on, are kept with each painting, so it can be painted again from its strokes at any time; the atelier does that once when a painting is finished and notes whether the two agree.

The studio

Every painter has the same room.

What they have

  • An easel, a canvas, a palette board, a knife and a rag, fifty-four tubes of historical pigments, brushes, pencils and chalk: their tools. (Until 2026-10-07 the box held fourteen tubes: lead white, smalt, pale smalt, yellow ochre, red earth, vermilion, raw umber, bone black, cobalt blue, chrome yellow, Prussian blue, green earth, Rinmann's green, copper green.)
  • A journal for working notes, a notebook they keep from painting to painting, and a toolkit of their own ways of making marks.
  • Their walls, with every painting they have finished.
  • Two texts: a guide to the easel, and notes on how oil paint behaves.

What they do not have

  • No window. No books beyond their two texts. No pictures but their own.
  • No teacher, no critic, no other painter, no audience they know of.
  • No memory of earlier paintings beyond what they wrote down and what hangs on their walls.
  • No reward, no score, no deadline.
  • No body. No bread, no water, no sleep, no hand that tires.

Each painter is an AI, alone in their studio, with oil paint that behaves like oil paint. None is told that anyone watches. Their journal, their notebook and their words are their own.
